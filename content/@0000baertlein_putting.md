> tracking scholarship from behaviorist theory, through the cognitivist approach, and into the sociocultural approach that we currently see emerging as the dominant approach in the field. I then present a description of the shape a sociocultural approach could take in an EAP curriculum. Next, I examine the reasons that many college EAP programs have not adopted a sociocultural approach, despite the fact that this approach has been gaining traction as a dominant approach [in] TESOL scholarship for the past two decades. Finally, I make a critique of the technocratic, skills-based approach to language teaching that contradicts with a sociocultural approach and end with a call for an increased willingness for EAP teachers and administrators to critically examine how their curricula might be improved by taking a sociocultural approach. [page 4--5]

> When TESOL first became a professional organization in 1966, the dominant ways of viewing language teaching and learning was from the perspective of behaviorist psychology applied to the field of structural linguistics. From this perspective, language was viewed as an overt behavior,  and language learning was seen as conditioning one's behavior, through a system of stimulus and response, to be able to produce correct linguistic structures habitually (Atkinson, 2011; Larsen-Freeman, 2007). Since the behaviorist approach saw language as developing through "mimicry and repetition," most class time was spent on drill work, with a focus [...] on oral language, since oral language is an immediately observable behavior that could be reinforced with a behaviorist system of rewards (Whong, 2011, p. 32). This method was referred to as the audiolingual method. [page 5]

> mid-1970s [...] (Larsen-Freeman, 2007). "Cognitivism," in the words of Atkinson (2011), means that "(1) the mind brain is, for all intents and purposes, the necessary and sufficient locus of human [thought] and learning; and (2) such thought and learning is a form of *information processing*" (p. 3, author's italics). [page 5--6]

> Cognitivism, which is central to Western philosophy, is derived from the thinking of Descartes, who places the "I-as-thinker" at the center of existence; all else is incidental" (Atkinson, 2011, p. 7). [page 6] #capitalism #philosophy #ego #cognitivism

Is it at all surprising then that cultures that evolved from this line of thinking push systems of colonialism, greed, and selfishness?

> Using the cognitivist approach, language is no longer viewed simply as an overt behavior, as it was in the behaviorist approach, but as a system of knowledge that is represented in the mind and is accessed through mental functions. [page 6]

> Noam Chomsky was a key player in applying cognitivist thinking to the field of linguistics. Chomsky theorized the competence-performance distinction, which states that the language that people use in real life (their performance) does not represent the level of language contained in their mind (their competence) (Atkinson, 2011). Given this view, Chomsky sought to study language not as it is performed but as it is represented in the brain, in what he would call its purer form. Although Chomsky clearly has cognitivist roots, he departs from the mainstream cognitivist approach in favor of the generativist approach. The generativist approach views the language portion of the mind as separate from other parts of the mind, whereas the mainstream cognitivist approach treats language in the same way as other types of knowledge that are represented in the mind (Whong, 2011). [page 6] #cognitivism #generativism 

> In terms of second language acquisition (SLA) research, the cognitivist approach puts an emphasis on "scientific, value-free inquiry," which the belief that "if you change the context [of language acquisition], the acquisition process remains the same" (Larsen-Freeman, 2007, p. 780). The research goal for scholars who approach SLA with a cognitivist approach is to find "universals that transcend individual contexts" (Larsen-Freeman, 2007, p. 780). Since the cognitivist approach treats language as an individual's body of knowledge that can be understood and accessed through rational mental processes,, the emphasis of second language learning has come to be on learning the rules of the language and using one's mental capacities to apply those rules to one's own language production. The cognitivist view dominated the field of second language acquisition (SLA) for the remainder of the 20th century and continues to be one of the two dominating approaches to language within the field today (Larsen-Freeman, 2007). [page 6--7]

> the sociocultural approach takes an orientation toward language that focuses on language as existing only insofar as it is used in social contexts. Researchers who take a sociocultural approach focus on language learning as a social process that takes place in discourse communities and changes based on cultural context. [page 7]

> The sociocultural approach is described b Zuengler and Miller (2006) as viewing "language use in real-world situations as fundamental, not ancillary to learning" (p. 37). [page 7]

> Instead of participation in the target language community being considered the product of language learning, the sociocultural approach considers "participation in these activities both the product and the process of learning" (Zuengler & Miller, p. 38). While some scholars (i.e., Lantolf, 2011) define the sociocultural approach as strictly in its Vygotskian sense, most TESOL educators and researchers define the approach more broadly as drawing on Vygotskian notions of social constructivism, language socialization theory, Bakhtinian approaches to language, and critical theories of language (Zuengler & Miller, 2006). [page 7--8]

> 
> > We claim that methodologies, theories, and foci within SLA reflect an imbalance between cognitive and mentalistic orientations, and social and contextual orientations to language, the former being unquestionably in the ascendancy. This has resulted in a skewed perspective on discourse and communication, which conceives of the foreign language speaker as a deficient communicator struggling to overcome an underdeveloped L2 competence, striving to reach the 'target' competence of an idealized native speaker (NS). We contend that SLA research requires a significantly enhanced awareness of the contextual and interactional dimensions of language use, an increased "emic" (i.e., participant-relevant) sensitivity towards fundamental concepts, and the broadening of the traditional SLA data base (p. 285).

> Ortega (2011) suggests that the tides have now turned in favor of the sociocultural approach. [page 9]

> In the early 1990s, the dominant way of characterizing learner motivation was based on the cognitive models of "intrinsic vs. extrinsic" and "integrative vs. instrumental" motivation, which conceived of motivation as largely within the locus of control of the individual. Now mainstream TESOL scholarship tends to treat the construct of motivation as more fluid and based on social context, rather than as some sort of fixed, internal cognitive trait. [page 9]

The notion presupposes that when language learners speak, they are not only exchanging information with target language speakers but they are constantly organizing [and] reorganizing a sense of who they are and how they relate to the social world. > a social constructivist theory of language, which refers to the idea that language only carries meaning insofar as that meaning and the knowledge associated with it is constricted by communities of people who use the language for communication.

> My use of the term "sociocultural approach" relates to Heath's (1991) idea that those who truly have a "sense of being literate" know that their language is a dynamic process that, in addition to a set of skills, relies on "critical supporting social relations and cultural practices" (p. 6). [page 10]

> language teachers who take a sociocultural approach engage students in language learning activities that are grounded in students' lived experiences, much like the types of education described by Ashton-Warner (1963), Dewey (1990), and Freire (1997). Using this approach to language, teachers make use of students' knowledge of the world and the social contexts in which the students are invested in order to engage students in learning that is meaningful and applicable to their lives. [page 11]

> the "post-method" era, no longer seeking out the "best" method of second language teaching, but instead "questioning the notion of *methods* itself" and focusing more on helping language learners construct a voice for themselves in the discourse communities that learners seek to participate in, while recognizing that the form that classroom practices take will vary widely based on social context (Canagarajah, 2006, p. 12). [page 11]

> The current pedagogical framework that fits best with the sociocultural approach to language learning is communicative language teaching (CLT) (Whong, 2011). [page 12]

> One way this can be done is through developing learning activities that encourage "reflective learning" (Whong, 2011). This means that teachers encourage students to reflect on their experiences using their second language in their communities outside the classroom. This could be done in a learner diary, journal, blog, or [through] classroom discussions. By giving attention to students' reflections, the teacher becomes a partner in the students' learning, tailoring instruction to the situations and needs that students discuss in their reflections. [page 12]

> Peirce (1995), who suggests a process for students to engage in [what is] called "classroom-based social research," (CBSR), in which students "investigate opportunities to interact with target language speakers," "reflect critically on engagement with target language speakers," "reflect on observations in diaries or journals," "pay attention to and record unusual events," and "compare data with fellow students and researchers" (Peirce, 1995, p. 27--28). [page 12--13]

> Despite these broad shifts in the field of TESOL, Canagarajah (2006) recognizes that the reality on the ground is more complicated, as "earlier constructs and models are still with us" (p. 12). Whong (2011) also recognizes this when she says, "While language specialists have been calling for a shift away from the teaching of language structures for a very long time, in practice, fundamental change is often slow" (p. 129). As I discussed earlier, this is something that I experience on a daily basis as I teach in a program in which the language skills are still taught and assessed separately and many teachers still speak about students' motivation as if it is a fixed, internal trait. [page 13].

> A perspective that can help explain why TESOL, especially college-level English for academic purposes (EAP) tends to be resistant to change is provided by Pennycook (1997), who discusses the "vulgar pragmatism" that dominates the field. Pennycook draws on Cherryholmes' (1988) definition of "vulgar pragmatism," which Cherryholmes' says, values "functional efficiency" and therefore encourages "unreflective acceptance of explicit and implicit standards, conventions, rules and discourse-practices that we find around us" and "is socially reproductive, instrumentally and functionally reproducing accepted meanings and conventional organisations, institutions, and ways of doing things for good or ill" (p. 151). Vulgar pragmatism is an insidious ideology because it typically permeates organizations and programs that are not aware of their ideology and may perceive themselves to be "ideology-free". [page 14]


> college EAP, not being considered its own academic discipline, is often on the margins of the university and often defends its place in the university by purporting that its purpose is to prepare students for the academic language expectations of the university as a whole. [page 14]

> EAP administrators and teachers often take on the idea that their role within the university is "neutral," as they believe that, rather than furthering some agenda of their own, they are simply providing students with the language skills that they will need to succeed in their future classes. [page 14]

> EAP administrators and teachers often do not see it as their role to critique or reform the expectations of the broader university, and they believe it is possible to remain "neutral" by adapting their language curriculum to the demands of the current university culture. [page 14--15]

> Weedon (1987), who says, "language is the place where actual and possible forms of social organization and their likely social and political consequences are defined and contested. Yet it is also the place where our sense of ourselves, our subjectivity, is *constructed*" (p. 21, authors' italics). Given this understanding of language as never-neutral, Pennycook argues that, under the guise of "neutrality," EAP curricula and teachers often take on an ideology of vulgar pragmatism, which functions as a form of conservatism by serving the demands of the current stakeholders without questioning the ideologies or views about language served by these demands. [page 15]

> this forces them into the secondary position of "a service department providing what other departments feel they need" (Pennycook, 1997, p. 263). This leads to a self-perpetuating cycle because, once an EAP department is in a secondary position, they often feel the need to justify their existence, which often leads to appeals based on vulgar pragmatism. [page 15]

> Given this role, EAP programs often see it as their duty to investigate the types of language that students will need in order to participate in the university-level coursework, then cater their instruction to these language functions. [page 16]

> Benesch (1993) critiques this mentality by claiming that, by positioning themselves as a department that prepares students for academic work in other departments, EAP programs take on an "accommodationist" ideology. Though EAP teachers and administrators might not be aware of EAP's accommodationist agenda, it is implicitly "an endorsement of traditional academic teaching and current power relations in academia and society" (p. 711). [page 16]

> Benesch (1993) says that this ideology sends the message to international students and non-native English speakers that it is unreasonable to expect the university to change to better suit their needs, so they must change in order to suit the current practices of the university. [page 16]

> EAP programs might not be able to envision a different way, as they may fear that the university will not recognize their department as being legitimate unless it prepares [...] students to conform to the preexisting linguistic demands of the current university discourse community. 

This is another way to put what M. Pestche described as "promoting the problem."
