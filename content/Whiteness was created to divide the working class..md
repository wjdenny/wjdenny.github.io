Whiteness was created to divide the working class. Whiteness has been assigned primarily to European ethnic groups, including European Jews[^2]. Theodore W. Allen argued that whiteness was created to divide the working class, pitting European-American laborers against African-American laborers[^1]. Ostensibly, this serves to increase competition, driving wages down and distracting workers from organizing against the capital-owning class. This effectively aligns the "white" working folk with the upper class and against other ethnic labor groups. This is captured in the Roland Wright (2004) paraphrase of John Steinbeck's (1960) *A Primer on the '30s*: "[...] the poor see themselves not as an exploited proletariat but as temporarily embarrassed millionaires."

As European immigrant labor began to identify itself as "white," so, too, did they develop attitudes of [[Anti-blackness is a distinct but related concept from white supremacy.|anti-blackness]][^3].

The expanding interpretations of whiteness over decades has functioned essentially as a shield protecting the upper class[^4]. This use of whiteness to divide the working class could also be why the "Great Replacement Theory" is such a threat to the upper class and driving legislative efforts against reproductive rights and protections on child labor. If labor is no longer white-majority, there is a chance for all laboring ethnic groups to organize together.

Further reading on racial capitalism:

Cedric Robinson:
	Black Marxism: The Making of the Black Radical Tradition (1983) - In this seminal work, Robinson explores the relationship between race, capitalism, and resistance, tracing the development of a Black radical tradition that critiques and challenges dominant capitalist structures.

Ruth Wilson Gilmore:
	Golden Gulag: Prisons, Surplus, Crisis, and Opposition in Globalizing California (2007) - Gilmore examines the political economy of incarceration in California, arguing that the expansion of the prison system is intertwined with processes of racial capitalism and economic globalization.

Robin D.G. Kelley:
	Race Rebels: Culture, Politics, and the Black Working Class (1994) - Kelley analyzes the history of African American resistance movements, highlighting the ways in which culture and politics intersect to challenge racial capitalism and advance social change.

Angela Y. Davis:
	Are Prisons Obsolete? (2003) - Davis argues for the abolition of prisons, critiquing the ways in which the prison-industrial complex perpetuates racial capitalism and systemic oppression, particularly for Black and brown communities.

David Roediger:
	The Wages of Whiteness: Race and the Making of the American Working Class (1991) - Roediger explores the construction of white identity and its role in shaping working-class politics and labor relations in the United States, highlighting the ways in which whiteness has been mobilized to maintain racial hierarchies within capitalist societies.
