## Notes
> *actions and behaviors that prioritize, uplift, amplify, and venerate white people and white culture **above** other cultures and communities*. [page 143]

> the 3 A's of Decentering Whiteness in the Workplace [page 143]

> The first step to decentering whiteness in the workplace is recognizing that the issue exists. [page 143]

Figure 11-1 shows four concentric circles with an arrow originating from the inner circle towards the right. The inner circle is "Whiteness is Centered." The next circle is "Awareness" and inside that circle are "Qualitative and quantitative employee data," "Educational workshops and talks," "Responding to workplace harm," and "Book club." The third circle is "Acknowledgement" and inside it contains "Public acknowledgement," "Internal acknowledgement," "Internal and external support," and "Specific policies to prevent harm." The final circle is "Accountability" and it contains "Employee feedback," "Leadership accountability," "System change," and "Communicate accountability progress." Outside the final circle to the right is "Whiteness is being actively decentered."

This illustrates the three steps of decentering whiteness: awareness, acknowledgement, and accountability.

> Data collection is a vital part of decentering whiteness. [page 144]

> An exorbitant amount of research claims that DEI trainings and workshops aren't effective. The general consensus is that these types of trainings are a waste of time and money. As we discussed in the previous chapters, the reason why this perception is so pervasive is because we're using a white measuring stick to evaluate these and other DEI interventions. [page 145]

One thing those claims forget is how those trainings affect non-white people.

> Education is a great preemptive tool for decentering whiteness in your workplace. You don't have to wait until a problem exists to address it. [page 146]

> I know there have been some protests against employee book clubs. A few weeks after the murder of George Floyd in 2020, Tre Johnson wrote a great piece in the *Washington Post* called "When Black People Are in Pain, White People Just Join Book Clubs." The premise of the article was that book clubs aren't enough to upend the hundreds of years of mistreatment that Black people in the US have endured. When reflecting on white people, Johnson wrote
> 
> > *what they do is never enough. This isn't the time to circle up with other white people and discuss black pain in the abstract; it's the time to acknowledge and examine the pain they've personally caused... white people tend to take a slow route to meaningful activism, locked in familiar patterns, seemingly uninterested in really advancing progress. Theirs is still a world of signs and signaling, where actions like joining book clubs... take precedence*.
> 
> [page 147]

> Outside of reading books and discussing them, what are we doing to put the information in them to use? I do think books can spark something in us that we hadn't previously realized, so I don't want to overlook their power, but I also don't want to promote overreliance on them. We can't fall into the trap of thinking that the books--or rather, our reading of them-- equates to "doing the work." Books are simply a tool you can use on your journey, but they're useless if you're not applying what you read to everyday life. [page 147--148]

> Don't ask your non-white colleagues to lead your book club unless they specifically request to. [page 148]

> Centering whiteness is an addiction that's hard to break free from. Because we're birthed into a world that centers whiteness, it can be challenging to divest from it, as my friend Joquina Reed would say. If we think of white-centering as an addiction that we're trying to overcome, it's helpful to look at the steps that those recovering from other addictions utilize. The first step in the popular twelve-step program is admitting that the problem exists. As an organization or institution, you must admit that whiteness is being centered in your workplace. [page 148]

> In 2009, the American Medical Association issued an apology specifically to Black physicians due to a century of "past wrongs." In 2021, the American Psychological Association (APA) issued a statement to publicly apologize to people of color for "promoting, perpetuating, and failing to challenge racism, racial discrimination and human hierarchy in the U.S." [page 149]

Harriet A. Washington, "Apology Shines Light on Racial Schism in Medicine," *New York Times*, July 29, 2008, https://nyti.ms/3Kk2G80.

"Apology to People of Color for APA's Role in Promoting, Perpetuating, and Flailing to Challenge Racism, Racial Discrimination, and Human Hierarchy in U.S.,' American Psychological Association, October 29, 2021, https://www.apa.org/about/policy/racism-apology.

> In early 2022, the editorial board of the *Baltimore Sun*, Maryland's largest newspaper, issued an apology as well, stating that for decades the publication "promoted polciies that oppressed Black Marylanders." [page 149]

*Baltimore Sun*, Editoral Board, "We Are Deeply and Profoundly Sorry: For Deades, the *Baltimore Sun* Promoted Policies That Oppressed Black Marylanders; We Are Working to Make Amends," *Baltimore Sun*, February 18, 2022, https://www.baltimoresun.com/opinion/editorial/bs-ed-0220-sun-racial-reckoning-apology-online-20220218-qp32uybk5bgqrcnd732aicrouu-story.html

> in January of 2023, the American Society of Human Genetics (ASHG) apologized for its participation in the eugenics movement. The ASHG supported a number of harmful policies, including forced sterilization, and supported the use of genetics to validate discrimination against the Black community. [page 149]

Rodrigo Pérez Ortega, "Human Geneticists Apologize for Past Involvement in Eugenics, Scientific Racism," *Science*, January 14, 2023, https://www.science.org/content/article/human-geneticists-apologize-past-involvement-eugenics-scientific-racism.

> While there's often a mad rush to bury the mistakes of forget that they ever occured, it's really powerful to be able to acknowledge where you've messed up or caused harm. [page 150]

> Having an anonymous feedback channel will let you know about smaller, seemingly more minor issues before they magnify into bigger problems. [page 151]

This would be useful to have in a class.

> *one of the most instrumental ways to decenter whiteness and foster an environment built on equity and inclusion is by having accountability measures in place and actually utilizing them.* [page 152]

> diversity targets are much easier to measure because if they're related to race, ethnicity, and gender, those are visible characteristics that are easier to assess. Objectives like inclusion, equity, and belonging are much more complicated to assess. [page 155]

> Don't be shy about sharing your goals and objectives for each new year or new quarter and also continuously providing information about progress. [page 155]

> Many workplaces were founded by known white supremacists; burning these organizations and institutions to the ground may not be realistic or feasible. Sustained system change is needed. [page 156]

> There are three areas you can assess to measure whether system change is taking place in your workplace: 1) resignation letters and exit interview data, 2) employee survey data and anonymous channels, and 3) HR reports. [page 156]
