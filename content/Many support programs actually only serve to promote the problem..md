Many support programs actually only serve to promote the problem. This is the case with many English for Academic Purposes (EAP) remedial programs and course sequences in US higher education. The problem those programs work on is that some international and immigrant students who do not speak English as a first language can struggle in academic programs. They try to solve this by requiring students who fall under a certain threshold of one or more English language proficiency tests to take one or more semesters (sometimes up to 2 and a half years) of additional coursework. This causes a lot of issues for students who only have funding for a certain number of years relative to the length of their program. However, academia politics and funding criteria incentivizes administrators of these programs to avoid looking deeper into the issue for a more fair solution and often favor proficiency tests that are invalid or bring in more students who would otherwise not actually need the extra classes. Gassam Asare ([[@2023gassamasare_decentering_dei#^ef659b|2023, p. 85--86]]) discusses this issue in the DEI field and affirms the real objective is to "make our jobs obsolete." This preference for creating a stable career path even at the expense of marginalized people may be why DEI programs, including EAP, [[DEI programs often miss opportunities to improve their organizations.|often miss opportunities to improve their organizations]] and those [[executive positions are overwhelmingly held by white people.]]

The issue of support programs in organizations promoting the problem rather than solving it is a complex one, with various factors at play. Brink (2014) highlights the paradox of gender equality programs, which can inadvertently perpetuate discrimination. Francesconi (2015) further complicates the issue by discussing how development programs can inadvertently promote rent-seeking behavior in farmer-based organizations. Schulberg (1979) and Hc (1979) both emphasize the importance of evaluating these programs to ensure they are effective and not just operating on rhetoric. These studies collectively suggest that a lack of evaluation, unintended consequences, and the perpetuation of existing power dynamics can all contribute to the problem-promoting nature of some support programs.
