Concept Introduction

Strengths of Narrative Therapy

  • Narrative therapy interventions excel at understanding
    • systems of oppression,
    • the influence of those systems on clients, and
    • the influence of clients on those systems.

Narrative therapy interventions work with personal, local, and dominant narratives, which gives us tools of social power analysis built into the framework.

Dominant Discourses

  • coordinate social behavior
  • assumed as truth
  • shoulds and goods

Dominant discourses are powerful stories and mores that exist in every community that coordinate social behavior. They are often taken-for-granted and assumed as truth. Most people go through their lives without ever questioning them. Dominant discourses tell us

  • how married people should act
  • what happiness should look like
  • what a good life looks like
  • what being a good parent looks like

Narrative therapy assumes that client problems arise from a mismatch of dominant narratives and lived experience.

Treatment Phases

Interventions

  • Separating the person from the problem
  • Examining dominant narratives
  • Developing preferred narratives
  • Highlighting exceptions to problem-saturated stories

Externalizing

The problem is the problem. The problem is not the person.

Similar to defusion in ACT, narrative therapists work to separate the client from the problem so that the client can engage with it and create new futures through narrative.

There are many ways to externalize a problem, from subtle linguistic shifts to more direct work.

Case Vignette

Your client is a 20yo female-identifying Sudanese university student and the first in her family to attend college, something she and her parents are proud of. Once on campus, though, she notices that classmates and even some professors sometimes make comments that leave her feeling out of place and doubting whether she really belongs. At the same time, she is responsible for helping raise her younger siblings back home in another city, often traveling back and forth to provide childcare and support. The weight of juggling these responsibilities has left her often anxious and unable to focus, with days where she feels low in energy and has little motivation to keep up with her daily routine.

  1. How would you externalize the problem?
  2. What questions could you ask to elicit unique outcomes?
  3. What alternative narratives could the client develop?

Time: 6 minutes

Participants could focus on either the gender dynamic in the parenting role or the racial power dynamics at school.

  2. Are there times at school where you do feel like you belong? Are there times when your family can take care of themselves without you?

Case Vignette

Your client is a 19yo white sophomore who began drinking heavily during his first year to feel more at ease in social settings. At first, it seemed like a way to make friends and forget about stress, but lately he finds himself drinking even when alone and skipping classes after long nights out. His grades have started to slip, and he worries that he is letting down his family, who sacrificed a lot to help him be the first in their household to attend college. He says he wants to cut back but feels stuck in the same cycle week after week.

  1. How would you externalize the problem?
  2. What questions could you ask to elicit unique outcomes?
  3. What alternative narratives could the client develop?

Time: 6 minutes

  1. What does alcohol do for you? How does alcohol help you achieve your goals?
  2. Tell me about a time you managed stress without alcohol. Tell me about a time you made friends without alcohol.
  3. a narrative of agency and control
