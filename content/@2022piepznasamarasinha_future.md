When I read statements like this, I feel denied. Like, I want to listen and understand, but I'm not allowed in these spaces. I think in some ways I'm wanting to be recognized as a "safe person" but that is problematic. I'm not always a safe person. I may try to be, but I have triggers that have been sheltered by privilege. Instead, I should seek out those BIPOC voices that consent to being public and heard, and learn from those. Whether or not I'm recognized as a "safe white person" is not under my control and not my concern if I'm focused on doing the work to make spaces where I have control and privilege more accessible and equitable. That said, some individuals may come to see me as a safe person to talk or vent to, and I should be ready to hold that space, listen, and honor their faith in me.

> "crip doulaing," a term created by disability justice organizer [[Stacey Park Milbern]] to describe the ways disabled people support/mentor newly disabled people in learning disabled skills (how to live on very low spoons, drive a wheelchair, have sex/redefine sexuality, etc.). A doula supports someone doing the work of childbirth; a crip doula is a disabled person supporting another disabled person as they do the work of becoming disabled, or differently disabled, of dreaming a new disabled life/world into being. This is a very Black and brown disabled, collective way of doing the work. [page 21]

[[Stacey Park Milburn]] and [[Leah Laksmi Piepzna-Samarashinha]], "Crip Lineages, Crip Futures," *Care Work* (Vancouver: Arsenal Pulp, 2018), 242--43.

### Part 1: Disability Justice in the End Times
#### "We Were Maybe Not Going to Save the World, but We Were Going to Save Each Other": How Disabled Mutual Aid is Different than Abled Mutual Aid

>disabled people are geniuses at staying alive despite everything. And we are often the only ones who remember each other and get in the trenches and kick ass to save each other when everyone else "forgets" about disabled people. [page 51]

Sandra Alland, Facebook, August 18, 2021, https://www.facebook.com/sandra.alland/posts/1734987846689065.

> When I look at the strategies we created, they can fall into roughly six categories: public campaigns and information sharing, crip research, crip resources share, crip checking in and collective care, creating disabled community spaces, and getting people money. [page 53]

> it's important to articulate that disability justice campaigns to share information and fight back against policies that would kill us overtly ARE mutual aid. [page 54]

> We shared recipes for homemade hand sanitizer and where to buy the fragrance-free kind, and made contact-free distro boxes for it on our blocks. [page 55]

> months before the CDC would say JUST KIDDING, PLEASE MASK, me and every crip I knew were wearing masks whenever we had to go into a grocery store or any other crucial inside place. People edged away from us. They thought we were sick, they thought we were crazy. They were right, but not in the way they thought. We were sick, crazy people who were determined to stay alive. [page 56]

> It was disabled people who first understood that viral load--the amount of virus one is exposed to-- is a factor in whether one might get COVID. [page 56]

> microcovid.org, a tool made by immunology and data nerds in San Francisco that helps you calculate the actual risk of daily activities based on your city and its current rates, type of mask worn and vax status, and how many people are around. We need to give ourselves credit, and get credit, for being the disabled and chronically ill citizen scientists and information sharers we are. [pages 56--57]

> friends later thanked me for being an early crip proponent of masking, that me talking about why I masked was probably why they had worn masks and were alive. [page 57]

> Crip Fund, and ad hoc group of disabled friends, mostly queer and mostly BIPOC, came together and raised money to give to disabled BIPOC in dire situations, via Venmo, no questions asked. [page 59]

"About Crip Fund," *Crip Fund*, updated June 20, 2020, https://cripfund.wordpress.com.

[page 60]

[[Deficit-based approaches are inherently ableist.]]

> while the word may have been created by anarchists, the practice of communities caring for each other has been practiced for millennia as a basic form of survival. As my friend and collaborator Ejeris Dixon points out in their essay "Fascists Are Using COVID-19 to Advance Their Agenda. It's Up to Us to Stop Them" the woman down the hall from me may never have used the term "mutual aid," but when she says, *I know you have asthma, I'm going wipe down the elevator with bleach twice a day*, she's practicing it. > large mutual aid collectives sprang up all over North America and beyond to meet the needs that were not being met by federal and state governments. These collectives were incredibly creative in their strategies to address people's needs on a massive scale, ranging from networks of people cooking and dropping off meals and masks to people they didn't know, to innovative new strategies, like the networks of free community fridges that sprung up on the sidewalks of Seattle and other cities. [page 62]

Maybe I can explore my issues with community by engaging in reciprocating with food -- I'm uncomfortable eating food other people prepared.

> Some of these mutual aid networks understood how mutual aid could easily turn into charity--with all the gatekeeping and barriers to accessing care and support, and "prove you're worthy and not scamming us, we'll tell you what you need and you better be grateful and accept it" ableist, racist, and classist ideologies that can infest many nonprofits-- and they worked hard against that. [page 62]
