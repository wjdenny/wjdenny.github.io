## Notes
> One of my favorite quotes comes from the Combahee River Collective, a group of Black feminists: "If Black women were free, it would mean that everyone else would have to be free since our freedom would necessitate the destruction of all the systems of oppression." That quote illustrates what the primary focus of workplace DEI efforts should be: removing systemic barriers for the most harmed and vulnerable population. [page 125]

Combahee River Collective, "The Combahee River Collective: A Black Feminist Statement," in *Capitalist Patriarchy and the Case of Socialist Feminism,* ed. Zillah R. Eisenstein (New York, Monthly Review Press, 1978), 362-72.

I sense something true in this, but I would like to see more reasoning behind it. Following the logic of "most marginalized," Black women could be further marginalized with sexual orientation, physical and cognitive abilities, etc. In addition, similar has surely been said about women's liberation in general from first wave feminists. I'm willing to bet that it's my own blindness or bias that is holding me back here, so I want to work out the argument fully.

> Are your Black employees more impacted by student loan debt? What resources can you provide to your Black employees who are affected by this and other types of debt? You could bring in experts to provide support around this particular issue. [page 139]

This what-about-me-ism reveals a deep white centeredness-- assuming that I should get support before any Black person-- see the "we" in the "Black"-- curb cut effect. also lack of trust that "all" includes Black.

My initial reaction is one of "what about me?" I also did not have access to generational wealth. I also have a significant amount of student loans. I do fully understand that these issues affect Black individuals at a much higher rate than white individuals, but if the promise of a curb-cut effect is taken seriously, the policy cannot be specifically supporting Black people, but specifically supporting the issues that Black people face disproportionately. For example, rather than paying off portions of student loan debt for Black employees, pay off a sliding scale, such that the more student loan debt someone has, the more assistance they get.  I may still be missing the point, but I'm struggling with this trust issue; a trust issue that is understandably on Black peoples' side as well. How many times have they heard "all" when it ended up excluding them?

> One *Harvard Business Review* article suggested allowing Black employees to "call in Black" when racial mega-threats occur against the Black community. [page 140]

Danielle D. King et al., "Give Black Employees Time to Rest and Recover," *Harvard Business Review*, February 22, 2021, https://hbr.org/2021/02/give-black-employees-time-to-rest-and-recover.

Again, I don't understand how this could have a curb-cut effect if a trans person cannot "call in trans" or a disabled person cannot take a "no spoons day" or a recent immigrant can't take a "culture shock day." Isn't the problem we want to avoid that we're privileging one centered category over others?
